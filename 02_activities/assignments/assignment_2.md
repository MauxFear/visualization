      ```
        Title: All the Biomass of Earth, in One Graphic
        source: https://www.visualcapitalist.com/all-the-biomass-of-earth-in-one-graphic/

        The infographic effectively tackles the challenge of representing the 
        vast and complex distribution of biomass on Earth, making it a strong 
        example of data visualization. Its primary strength is its aesthetic 
        quality; the use of detailed 3D illustrations rendered as blocks is 
        visually striking and highly engaging, drawing the viewer into the 
        data. The layout is clean, and the breakdown of information is logical, 
        starting from the total animal biomass and drilling down into kingdoms 
        and specific taxonomic groups. The substantive quality is also high. It 
        presents a significant dataset clearly, labelling each category with 
        its value and providing concise, informative descriptions. 
        Crucially, it highlights key takeaways, such as the dominance of plants 
        and bacteria and the surprisingly small contribution of humans, 
        effectively conveying the core message. Sources are clearly cited, 
        adding credibility.
        However, the visualization's main weakness could be related to its 
        perceptual quality, specifically the use of 3D volumes. As discussed in 
        visualization literature and in class, the use of 3D charts can be 
        detrimental to data perception and the conveying of the message. While 
        the 3D volumes represent the quantities, it's difficult to visually 
        confirm the precise proportional differences compared to a 2D chart. 
        This statement holds true based on established visualization 
        principles, as our perception struggles with accurately judging 3D 
        volumes on a 2D plane. However, in the specific case of this 
        visualization, this limitation might be considered more acceptable due 
        to the enormous range and scale of the data presented.
        The biomass values span multiple orders of magnitude, from Plants at 
        450 Gt C down to Wild Birds at 0.002 Gt C, a difference of over 200,000 
        times. Representing such vast disparities accurately and clearly on a 
        single, linear-scale 2D chart, like a simple bar chart, is inherently 
        challenging. Tiny values like humans or wild mammals would become 
        almost invisible next to giants like plants or bacteria, potentially 
        obscuring one of the visualization's key messages: the relative 
        insignificance of certain groups despite their prominence in our 
        perception.
        Therefore, while the 3D block approach sacrifices perceptual precision 
        for fine comparisons between similarly sized categories (e.g., 
        accurately judging Fish vs. Marine Arthropods by eye), it excels at 
        conveying the immense differences in scale across the entire dataset. 
        Despite these perceptual limitations, the visualization is classified 
        as "good" because its exceptional aesthetic appeal and strong 
        substantive content effectively capture attention and communicate the 
        overall scale and distribution of biomass. The clear labelling 
        compensates significantly for the inherent imprecision of visually 
        comparing 3D volumes, ensuring the core information is accurately 
        conveyed while at the same time allowing a comparison between different data scales. This visualization succeeds in making complex scientific 
        data accessible and memorable for a broad audience.

      ```
